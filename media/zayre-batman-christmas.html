Zayre's Batman Christmas Commercial

Commercial · 1986

About

Holy Mistletoe! Inside Zayre’s Campy 1986 Batman Christmas Commercial

Long before the gritty cinematic blockbusters of today, Batman and Robin once took a late-night break from fighting crime to go holiday shopping. In December 1986, the regional discount department store chain Zayre launched a memorable, high-budget television commercial featuring the Caped Crusaders. While the ad was designed to promote the store's extended 24-hour holiday hours, it quickly became famous for its meticulous, nostalgic replication of 1960s pop culture—and the fierce celebrity lawsuit that followed it.

The Plot: A 3:00 A.M. Retail Emergency

The commercial opens with a faithful recreation of the classic 1966 Batman television aesthetic, complete with bright colors, dramatic camera angles, and the iconic, campy atmosphere.

The narrative setup features a highly relatable holiday panic:

  • Inside the Batcave, the Dynamic Duo suddenly look at the clock and realize it is 3:00 a.m. on Christmas Eve.
  • In a moment of sheer panic, they realize they have completely forgotten to buy Christmas presents.
  • To solve their crisis, they sprint to a 1960s-style Batmobile and race off into the night, heading toward a local Zayre department store that is open 24 hours a day for last-minute shoppers.

As they speed out of the Batcave, the actor portraying Robin delivers a sequence of signature, deadpan catchphrases, exclaiming, "Holy mistletoe!" and later, "Holy rock around the clock!" Once inside the brightly lit Zayre store, the duo browses the retail aisles while debating what kind of present they should purchase for that "pesky prankster," the Penguin.

Recreating the 1966 Magic Without the Original Stars

What made the commercial stand out to audiences was its incredible attention to detail. The advertising agencies behind the spot (including BBDO) deliberately bypassed the dark, serious tone of the 1980s comic books to pay direct homage to the beloved live-action television show.

However, the producers did not hire the original actors, Adam West and Burt Ward. Instead, they cast lookalike actors. These performers did not merely wear the unmistakable, brightly colored costumes; they precisely mimicked the highly specific acting mannerisms of the original stars. The actor playing Batman perfectly mirrored West’s distinct, deadpan vocal delivery, rigid posture, and dramatic, over-the-top hand gestures.

The Aftermath: From Christmas Cheer to Burbank Courtroom

The commercial successfully captured the holiday spirit, and the advertising agencies had legally covered their bases regarding corporate intellectual property by purchasing the proper character licensing rights directly from DC Comics.

However, Adam West was furious when the ad began broadcasting. Believing that the producers had deliberately appropriated his unique creative identity and personal acting style to confuse audiences into thinking he was under the cowl, West filed a $900,000 lawsuit in California against the ad agencies and the department store chain.

The resulting legal battle stretched into the end of the decade, serving as a landmark case study regarding the intersection of an actor's personal right of publicity and a studio's corporate copyright ownership.

Ultimately, the case culminated in January 1990 at the Burbank Superior Court. Judge Stephen O'Neil dismissed the lawsuit, ruling against the veteran actor. The court determined that under West's original 1965 television contract, he had signed away all rights to his specific portrayal of the character. Judge O'Neil famously remarked that while West was undeniably a talented actor, he was an interpreter of the character, not the creator of Batman. Because DC Comics legally held the trademark, they maintained the ultimate authority to license those exact character traits out to third-party advertisers.

Though the Zayre department store chain dissolved just a few years later, the brief moment where Gotham's protectors rushed out for late-night retail deals remains a legendary footnote in television broadcast history.
